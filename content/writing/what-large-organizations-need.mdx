Large organisations do not fail for lack of clever engineers. They fail because cleverness is the wrong currency once the system has real constraints: regulation, legacy, politics, money already spent, and a production estate that cannot be paused for a rewrite.

What they need is judgment under those constraints. The ability to see which problem is actually load-bearing, which abstraction will become a trap, and which "modernisation" is just a more expensive way to keep the same mess.

## Cleverness is cheap. Constraints are not.

A brilliant design that cannot be operated by the people who will inherit it is not brilliant. A migration that ignores the settlement, tax, or safety path is not a migration. It is a demo with a blast radius.

The engineers who become indispensable in these environments are rarely the loudest in a design review. They ask what happens when the vendor is late, when the data is wrong, when two source-of-truth systems disagree, when the person who knew the system leaves.

## The work is translation

You are translating between what the business believes it bought and what the system can actually do. That requires technical depth and a refusal to hide behind jargon. It also requires the humility to keep software smaller than the organisation's ability to run it.

If you want to be useful at that scale, stop optimising for elegance in isolation. Optimise for decisions that still look correct after contact with operations, audit, and time.
